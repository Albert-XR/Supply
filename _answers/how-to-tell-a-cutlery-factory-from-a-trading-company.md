## Factory vs trading company: what actually changes for a buyer

| Dimension | Factory | Trading company |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls the production schedule | The supplier's own plant | A third-party producer |
| Specification changes | Decided and implemented in-house | Relayed to the producer |
| Tooling and new handle shapes | Owned and maintained on site | Dependent on the producer |
| Finish consistency across reorders | Same line and same process can be re-run | May change if the producer changes |
| Quality records | Can be tied to your own production run | Usually generic or from another party |
| Factory audit / live video walkthrough | Available on request | Usually not available |

The distinction matters most when something goes wrong or when you need to repeat an order two years later. A factory can re-run the same pattern and finish because it still holds the tooling and process; an intermediary has to find a plant willing to reproduce it.

## Five checks before you commit

1. **Legal identity** — the company name on the business licence, the registered factory address, and the name on the shipping documents should match. A name that reads like an export agent rather than a manufacturer is worth a follow-up question.
2. **Capacity evidence** — monthly output in pieces, headcount and production lines, compared with your order size. Vague answers here are the most common signal of brokered production.
3. **Tooling ownership** — who holds the moulds for handle shapes and who maintains them. A factory that owns tooling can answer immediately and show the mould room.
4. **Quality records tied to your run** — sample confirmation, pre-shipment inspection and, on request, third-party testing or a factory audit. See [how to check cutlery quality before shipment](/answers/cutlery-quality-check-before-shipment/) for the four-step process.
5. **A live look at the floor** — an on-site visit or a live video walkthrough on a working day. This is the single check an intermediary cannot simulate.
